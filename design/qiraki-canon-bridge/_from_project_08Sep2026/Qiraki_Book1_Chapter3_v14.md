# ENLIGHTENED: THE ACADEMY ERA, BOOK 1, "THE KING AND THE CLIMB"

## Chapter Three: The Standard Message

*No cadet fails alone. No cadet succeeds alone either. Read that twice.*

*Assembly-hall signage, posted for first-week orientation*

Denic is awake before the ring on Tuesday and it puts the whole morning wrong.

He's sitting up on his bunk with both legs over the side and the band already on, and he's been there long enough that the light comes up on him mid-sentence.

"It works through the wall."

"What does."

"The band. I've been down the corridor and back." He holds his wrist up like the wrist proves something. "Two doors down, into the stairwell, and it still put the map up. Nothing dropped out of it."

"What time did you go into the stairwell."

"I want to say four."

I get my boots on. The collar clasp takes me one try this morning, and Denic's is already turned under itself at the back again on the same side as yesterday, which means it isn't the clasp.

The corridor moves like it did yesterday, faster now. The kid with the crest still ducks the hatch frame and two kids behind him duck it now as well, and neither of them needs to.

Mess hall smells the same as well. The line hands me a tray and I recognize two of the three now, and the third one is grey and holds its shape when you push it.

Corw's already at the table when I get there, one boot up on the bench, mid-argument with the taller of the two Hiopi. Ilyen's at the end with her tray squared to the table edge and both hands flat, eating in small amounts, not looking at either of them.

Their side of the bench is folded out the same as yesterday, set to a different height under each of them this time, the taller one's a notch up from the smaller one's. First time I've noticed it does that.

I sit down and the taller one's mid-word, and about a third of the way through that word it turns into language.

It comes into my head the way my own name did yesterday. There's no sound in the room that wasn't already in it, the low register still going through the air across the table, and on top of it, arriving somewhere behind that, a plain flat voice saying words I know in an order I know. The two don't line up. His mouth is a half beat ahead of what I'm hearing and the shape it makes has nothing to do with the words.

I look at my tray instead.

"…my sister's year had eleven," he's saying, to Corw. "Nine placed. Two went home in the first hour and one of those was my cousin."

Denic drops in next to me and takes half my tray without asking. Which I let him do today.

"Went home meaning what," he says, catching up to a conversation that started without him.

"Meaning went home." The Hiopi takes a piece off his own tray with two fingers and puts it away whole. "You don't have to stay in it. You have to start in it."

"And if you stay in it," Corw says.

"Then you're in it until you're not."

"Okay," Denic says. "Say I'm in it. Say I stay in it. What's the actual thing that happens."

"Everyone in the city, all at once, in one place, and nobody carries anything." The Hiopi turns one hand over on the bench, palm up, and looks at it a second like he's checking it's steady. "Last one still standing wins the city. One winner. Everyone else places, and where you place is what you have for a year."

"That's it? Last one standing?"

"That's it."

"That's a stupid rule," Corw says.

"It's a simple rule."

"It's stupid. What happens if two of you are the same."

"Then it goes longer."

Corw doesn't have anywhere to take that, so he takes it somewhere else. "How many are usually in it."

"For a city, thousands. For a place this size, less."

"This size." Denic's got his elbows on the table now. "Wait. Here counts?"

"Anywhere with enough of us counts. A carrier counts. This counts." He says it the way Corw says his own age. "That's why I came here and not somewhere better."

"Counts for what, though." Denic still hasn't let it go. "What do you actually get."

"Placement. Standing. What you get for a year." He doesn't look up from his tray. "The real one's not this. You get that once you're old enough for it to matter, and it doesn't look anything like the academy."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer you're getting."

The smaller one hasn't said anything since I sat down. She puts one hand flat on the floor and shifts her weight off it, and when she talks the flat voice arrives in my head a half beat behind her.

"The year-end exam is inside it here."

"Inside what," Denic says.

"Inside the ritual. They fold it in." She goes back to her tray. "It's better."

"Better how?"

"Better odds."

Corw's already gone back to the two-of-you-are-the-same question, building a whole scenario out of it where he wins something. Denic argues with the scenario instead of the rule underneath it. The actual odds never come up. The taller Hiopi answers about half of what Corw throws at him.

Then Ilyen gets up without a word, comes around behind us, hooks two fingers into the back of Denic's collar, and turns it down where it belongs.

"That's the last one," she says. Doesn't look at him. Sits back down and goes back to eating.

Denic's hand goes to his own neck a half second too late to have caught it himself.

"The last what."

She doesn't answer. She's already looking at something else.

Then the band puts my mother in my head.

It doesn't announce her. There's a small mark in the corner of my eye that wasn't there and then her voice is inside my ear from the wrong side of my ear, and she's already three words in when I catch up to it.

"…and it holds pressure, so that's fine, that's not what I'm calling about."

There's a compressor running behind her, the one from four rings down, the knock in it landing off-time every third stroke where the mount's gone soft.

"Bonus cleared for finishing the pin job same-day, so that's covered. The fridge holds more than it used to. That's one way to know you're gone."

A picture opens over my tray, about the size of both hands, hanging there. The courier drone on our floor with its undercarriage back on and the fault light dead and the whole of it sitting square on its skids. Her boot is in the bottom of the frame. She hasn't noticed her boot is in it.

"Got the contract for the strip too. Starts Wednesdays. So don't go worrying about me, I've got it handled."

The picture changes. The tool wall, all of it, hung by size, and it takes me a second to work out why she'd send that and then I don't get any further with it than that.

"Eat what they give you even if it's grey. Especially if it's grey."

The picture changes again. Nothing in this one. The panel over the stove with the grey sheet patch on it, and the strip of light off it, and no people in the frame anywhere.

"That's all. That's everything. See you."

It ends. The mark in the corner of my eye moves and opens something else, and the flat voice comes back on top of where her voice was.

*Reply. Standard message, or compose.*

Compose puts a field up in the air over my tray, a blank one, waiting, with the trace-guide sitting under it the same way it sits under the patterns in class. Twenty-one letters. I have four.

I take standard. The flat voice reads it out as it fills in.

*Dear Calder family. Your cadet is well. Your cadet is training. Your cadet will write again when there is something worth telling you.*

*Signature.*

I put my name on it. It takes my name off the band rather than out of my hand, which makes it quicker than anything else this band has asked me for today.

*Sent.*

"…so then it's not a rule, it's a preference," Denic's saying, "and you can't lose a fight on a preference."

"You can lose a fight on anything," Corw says.

Ilyen puts her tray on top of Denic's and stacks both of them on top of mine, in that order, without being asked and without looking up, and goes.

Tuesday is two engineering blocks and one rune session, which Denic reads off the map and reports to the table like news.

The bench room is colder than it was yesterday, or I'm in it earlier. The rack down the back wall has the tools on it and under the tools there's a second rack I didn't look at yesterday, and this one has the actual units on it, forty or fifty of them, laid on their sides in rows with their output shafts all pointing one direction.

She's at our bench before I am. There's a unit on the bench between her hands, a real one, off the rack, and she's got it turned so the housing seam faces her.

"Reaka," she says. "Reqa." Same speed, same weight, no gap in the middle of it. She says it before I've said anything, which means she's doing it because I walked up, not because I got it wrong.

"You did that yesterday too, Reqa. Same both times. I never even got it wrong either time."

She looks at me for a second. "Maybe later."

"We'll see." She turns the unit a quarter over. "They're letting us have the real ones from today. Everyone gets one and everyone puts it back on the rack at the end."

The machine at the front of the room says most of that about a minute later, to the room, at length. She's already got the first bolt out.

The real one weighs. That's what's different at first. The shape hanging over the bench yesterday came apart with a finger and held still wherever she left it, and this one wants to roll, and the housing halves want to come off crooked, and the whole of it is cold enough off the rack that it takes the warm out of my hands in about a minute.

It's the same unit. Everything on it is where it was in the light. The pin seat's under the collar where it was under the collar yesterday and I get two fingers in and find it without going around the outside first.

She sees me do it. She doesn't say anything about it, and what she does instead is stop telling me which piece is which. She takes the housing half off and holds it out sideways without looking and I take it, and she goes into the gear train, the next piece coming back just as smooth.

The gear train is the part I've never had a good look at. My mother's had these apart on our own floor and it was always the fast version, the version where you're paid for the hour, get the fault, get it closed, get it out the door. This one comes apart in stages and stays apart on the bench in front of me, and the reduction sits there in daylight, small gear driving big, three times over, speed going out of it and force coming back in at every step, and at the end of it a screw thread the length of my thumb, longer than anything else on the bench.

"That's the whole trick," she says, when I've had my hand on the thread for longer than I meant to. "Everything else in here is one of six things. Here, this screw."

"What's it do."

"Same as any of them. Just turns force into a push instead of a hold." She takes it off me. "Same six every time. They just get smaller and there's more of them."

We get it apart twice. The second time is faster and I do more of it, and she stops holding her hand out for pieces because I've started putting them down in the order they came off, which is what she was doing yesterday and I only worked out that was deliberate this morning.

The third time we put it back together and I close the housing, four bolts, round it one to the next, going home on each one before I move to the next.

She picks the unit up and turns the output by hand.

Then she turns it again, slower, and stops it about a third of the way around, and holds it there.

"Tight."

"It's not tight, it's cold."

"It's tight in one place." She rolls it back and forward across the same short stretch, twice. "Once around. Feel it."

I feel it. It turns clean for most of the way and then there's a stretch about the width of two fingers where it wants more out of me than the rest of it does, and then it lets go again.

"There's nothing in it," I say. "I put it in the same. It came out clean and it went in the same."

"Nothing's in it. I see that now."

"Then it's the shaft."

"The shaft was clean when it came out." She sets it back down on the bench and puts one finger on the first bolt, and then moves the finger around the four in the order I did them. She's got it exactly. She watched the whole time and I never saw her look up. "You did them like that."

"That's how you do them."

"That's how you do them on a housing that's already sprung." She backs the first bolt off, then the one next to it, then the next, all four, in the same circle I put them in, and the housing halves come loose against each other with a small dry sound. "This one's new. There's no slack anywhere in it to take that up, so it only holds the bore round if the four come down together. You pulled one corner all the way down on its own and the bore went oval, and it's been sitting oval since you closed it."

She snugs all four by hand, finger tight, no driver.

"Now across."

She goes first bolt, then the one diagonal from it, then the third, then its diagonal, a quarter turn on each and then around again, a quarter turn on each, and again, and the driver never stays on one bolt long enough to finish it. It takes four times as long as what I did. She hands it to me at the end without saying anything.

I turn the output. It goes around, and around again, and there's nothing in it anywhere.

"How did you know that from the outside."

"I didn't, not from the outside. I broke my first one the same way." She's got the next one off the rack before I've set the last one down. "Put it on the rack and get another one. You're doing the closing."

I close six of them before the session ends. The fourth one I do without thinking about it and it comes out clean, and she doesn't check that one, which I notice about a minute after she doesn't do it.

The second engineering block is the same as the first, a different unit and a scored thread in it that neither of us caused. The rune session after lunch runs the four patterns and goes fast because it's the only one today.

Wednesday is four hours of rune-patterning and the room finds out what four hours is.

Same room, same desks, same two hundred of us, same machine standing where a teacher would stand. Corw's got the desk in front of mine before I even sit down. Ilyen's back on my other side, same spot as the first day. Denic on my left, same as always. No new pattern. First, Second, Third, Fourth, and then First, Second, Third, Fourth, and the light draws it and my hand follows it and the sound lands in my head, four sounds in an order, over and over, until the sounds stop being sounds and turn into a shape my arm makes.

Somewhere in the second hour the room starts inventing.

Denic gets his left hand working. It takes him about forty minutes and by the end of it he can run all four with it, badly, and the band takes them all, which he takes as a personal victory over the band.

Corw does his with his eyes shut and gets three out of four, and does it again and gets three out of four, and the one he misses is the Fourth both times.

Two rows up a kid works out you can trace faster than the light if you already know where the light's going, and inside ten minutes half the room is racing the light, and about a third of those traces don't take because the band wants the whole path and not the ending.

Ilyen isn't racing anybody. She runs all four at the pace she ran them yesterday, one after another, with about a second between, and does it again, and doesn't stop.

Corw counts her at some point in the third hour because he counts everything.

"She's done ninety."

"Ninety what," Denic says.

"Ninety sets. Since the break. That's three sixty."

"You've been sitting there counting her."

"I've been sitting there counting everybody." He says it flat, not caught. "I'm at two hundred and forty and I'm the second most in this row."

Denic leans across me to look at Ilyen, and Ilyen doesn't look up, and her hand is already on the First again.

"Are you all right?"

"Yes."

"You've done three hundred and sixty of them."

"Three hundred and sixty-five."

Denic leans back into his own seat and puts both hands over his face.

Corw twists around in his seat. "You're the only one in the row who hasn't missed once."

"I'm busy," I say, not looking up. My face does something I don't have a name for.

There's script on the wall down at the front of the room, over where the machine stands, a full line of it running most of the width of the wall and cut into the surface rather than printed on. I've looked at it four times today. It's shapes with straight edges, some of them with a corner in, most of them apart from each other, some of them shapes I've been drilling all week.

I look at it and then I look back at my own hands, because the light's moving again.

By the fourth hour Denic's stopped talking, which is the first time that's happened since I met him, and Corw's stopped counting, and the kid two rows up who was racing the light has put his head down on the desk in front of it. The room goes quiet for a stretch that goes long enough that I hear the ventilation.

The one engineering block on a Wednesday comes at the end of it.

She's at the bench with a unit already open and she doesn't look up when I get there, and her jaw is set in a way it hasn't been either of the other two days.

"Four hours."

"How many'd you do."

"Eight hundred and sixty." She gets the housing half off harder than she needs to. "The same four. Again."

I've been thinking about this since the second hour, which is longer than I've ever thought about a sentence before saying it.

"If they let us trace them backward we'd be done in half the time."

She stops with the driver in her hand and looks at me for about a second, which is a second more than the line earns.

"It'd be the same number," she says, and goes back into the housing.

Noted.

Thursday is two engineering blocks and one rune session, and I run the four bolts across without thinking about it now, and by the second unit I've stopped putting the pieces in a row on the bench because I've stopped needing to look at them.

Thursday evening the common room is loud in the way it's loud every evening, which is to say sixty of us in a space built for forty, and no one has worked out yet that the benches by the far wall are the good seats.

Room lights up at once.

Mine goes first, it's on my wrist and everything of mine goes first. It throws a shape up into the air over the middle of the floor, and then there are sixty of them, sixty copies of the same man standing in the air at sixty different heights, overlapping through each other, some at chest height where a kid's sitting on the floor, some up near the ceiling. Where they cross it goes bright and you can't see any of him at all.

A voice comes down out of the ceiling before he says anything. It's a woman's voice and it's already halfway through something else.

"Sit down for this. Second-years, this is not yours, you've had it. First-year intake, sit down."

Sixty of us sit down on the floor. Denic sits on my left and Corw arrives from somewhere and sits on my right, and Ilyen is already sitting against the wall behind us and has been the whole time.

The man in the air has a face I stop looking at almost immediately. Middle-aged, ordinary, a face that turns up behind a desk in an office you go to for a form. He waits until the room stops moving, and then a little bit longer than that.

"Good evening."

He says it quietly. Two rows in front of me a kid leans forward.

"My name is Aurelian Kest. I hold oversight for this cluster, eleven academies, yours one of them. Twenty-two thousand and six of you started somewhere in it this month."

Denic looks at me and mouths *eleven*.

"Twenty-two thousand," Corw says out loud.

"Stuff it," somebody hisses behind him. Corw goes quiet, still sitting exactly where he was the whole time.

"Some of you will pull out." Kest doesn't change his volume for it. "Don't let that discourage you. Persevere."

The man in the air doesn't move when he says it.

"I'm glad you're here. The Coalition thanks you for the work ahead of you, and I thank you for choosing the Synker and Mek branches of the Circle Academy system. I wish you all a good meal."

Sixty copies of him go out together, all at once, and the floor is just floor again.

The voice out of the ceiling comes back in about four seconds, at the volume it uses for corridor announcements.

"First-year intake. Kit issue completes tomorrow. Any cadet still without a second set of greys reports to the supply office before zero-eight. Overflow rooming is being resolved this month, four to a room becomes two, do not move your own bunk, you will be told. Combat instruction Friday, zero-seven hundred, pilot track only, Instructor Vantree, and Instructor Vantree does not hold the door."

The room comes apart into sixty separate conversations before the ceiling's finished.

"Two to a room," Denic says, straight at me, before anyone else has got a word out. "Overflow. That's not us. We're two."

"We were always two."

"Yeah, but now we're *officially* two." He's up on his knees. "Some poor kid down the corridor is getting moved out of somebody's room this month and I have never in my life been so pleased about somebody else's problem."

"He wasn't talking to us anyway," Corw says.

"He said twenty-two thousand, that's us, we're in the twenty-two thousand."

"He said some of you'd 'pull out.'" Corw gets up off the floor and stretches like the floor was the hard part of the evening. "That's the bottom of it. That's always the bottom of it. He's telling the bottom of it to keep up."

"That's not what he said."

"It's what he meant. They say it in the recruiting too. It's a thing they say." Corw's looking past us at the far side of the room now. "My uncle got it word for word and he's a Tank instructor on Carabil now, so."

"Your uncle."

"Ask the clinic."

"That doesn't work for everything," Denic says. "You can't just say ask the clinic."

Corw goes. Denic watches him get about six strides and stop to say all of it again to somebody else, which is what he did on Monday, in the corridor, in the same order.

"He's going to be like this for years," Denic says. He sounds satisfied about it.

Behind us Ilyen gets up off the wall. She's got her band off and turning over in one hand, and she stops next to us on her way past, which she hasn't done before.

"He meant it," she says.

She doesn't say which part. She goes on past us and out through the door at the far end, around the outside of everything, the way she goes everywhere.

Dinner runs in the main hall instead of the mess, real plates instead of trays, and there's meat on mine that isn't grey and holds a shape I recognize. Denic eats like he's making up for four days of it in one sitting. Nobody explains why tonight's different.

He's face-down on his bunk with one boot off before I've got the light out, gone like the switch got thrown.

I take the band off and the room comes back to being the room, and then I put it on again, because the map's in it.

The academy hangs over my bunk in the dark, small enough to fit between my hands, turning. My dormitory picks itself out brighter than the rest of it. The line runs out of the dormitory, across the yard, past the shape of the mech in the yard, and stops at the building at the far edge that I still haven't walked to. The same flat voice reads the line off for me.

*Combat instruction. Friday. 0700.*

Fourteen hundred and some meters of yard, going by the map, which explains why we start at seven and not six. I've walked further than that to a strip that sold parts.

I put the band on the shelf above the drawer. The line stays where it was for a while after I close my eyes, off to the left, in the dark, which is not how any of this is supposed to work.
