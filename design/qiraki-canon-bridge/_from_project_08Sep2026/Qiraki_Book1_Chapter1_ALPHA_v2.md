### POV: Trav Calder

*“****Talent knows no station. Every cadet who walks through these gates does so on merit alone, and merit alone will carry them as far as ambition allows.****”* — Cradle Circle enrollment notice, standard text, one copy delivered to every subsidized household in the district

My boots have new soles on them and my mother hasn’t said anything about it.

They didn’t have new soles last night. I know because I checked them twice before I went to bed, the way you check something you’re going to need in the morning even when there’s nothing wrong with it, and both heels were worn down past the tread on the outside edge where I walk crooked. Now they aren’t. Now there’s fresh rubber cut a little proud of the leather at the toe, still tacky when I press a thumb into it, and the whole flat smells faintly of the adhesive she uses on gasket work.

She’s at the counter with her back to me, sleeves already pushed up past the elbow at an hour when there’s no work to do.

“Sit down,” she says. “There’s egg.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“I didn’t ask you that.”

So I sit down, and I eat the egg, and I don’t say anything about the boots, because there isn’t a version of that conversation where either of us comes out of it better than we went in. She’d tell me the soles were going anyway. I’d say they had another season in them. We’d both be lying at each other across a table for no reason at all on the last morning I’m going to be sitting at it.

The flat is four rooms and I can see three of them from where I’m sitting. The stove panel she never replaced, the corner of it lifted where the clip broke. The wrench set on the wall hung by size, because she can’t stand a drawer she has to dig through, and because I’ve been hanging them back in order since I was old enough to reach the low end of the rack. The couch with the burn in the arm from a soldering iron that neither of us has ever admitted to. Twelve years of my life in here, and it takes about four seconds to look at all of it.

She turns around with her mug and catches me doing it, and she doesn’t say anything, which is its own kind of saying something.

“They give you a list,” she says.

“I packed off the list.”

“Show me.”

I open the bag on the floor and she goes through it standing over me, not touching anything, just moving her eyes down the contents the way she goes over a rig before she signs off on it. Two sets of the grey they mailed us, folded badly. The soft cap. Wash kit. The card with my name on it that I can’t read, in a sleeve, with a cord so it can hang around a neck. That’s all of it. They were very clear that it’s all of it.

“You’ll have less than the ones who show up with more,” she says. “That evens out faster than you’d think.”

“Okay.”

“It does.” She says it a second time like the first one didn’t take, and then she lets it go.

We walk out at half past five and she pulls the door shut behind us without locking it, which she never does, and I decide not to ask.

The strip is still shuttered the whole way down. Six repair shops between our door and the transit stop, all of them closed, all of them still smelling of solder and cutting oil, because the smell doesn’t leave the block just because the doors are down. Sheet-metal awnings hang low enough over the walkway that my mother ducks the third one without looking up, the same duck she’s been doing since before I was born, at an awning that’s only actually low for about two strides. Above the gap where the two towers lean in close, a cargo hauler crosses the sky sideways and slow, engines throttled down to the low grinding note they use over residential ground, and I don’t look up at it, because I have never once in my life looked up at one of those.

Two shop owners are out early enough to see us. Neither of them says anything. They nod, the particular short nod that means they know exactly whose kid is leaving this morning and don’t intend to make a performance of it, and my mother nods back the same way. Nobody on this street has ever needed more words than that for anything that mattered.

The transport is already sitting at the stop with the ramp down. It’s a freight lifter with a crew ladder welded on, hull scarred grey, four other kids standing around near it holding bags exactly like mine and doing the same work with their faces that I’m doing with mine. One of them has a woman’s arm around his shoulders and he’s leaning into it hard, and I look away from that.

My mother stops about ten paces short of the ramp.

“Here,” she says, and presses something into my palm before I’ve seen what it is.

It’s a socket. The smallest in her set, the size that doesn’t fit anything I have ever watched her put it on, worn bright on one flat where a hand has held it a great deal longer than any job needed.

“What’s it for?”

“Nothing yet.” She’s already stepping back. She’s got the look she gets when a job’s closed out and she’s moving to the next one before she lets herself feel finished with the last. “Put it somewhere you won’t lose it. Not the bag. Somewhere with a door on it.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No.” She reaches up and flattens my collar with two fingers, quick, businesslike, the way she’d square a plate before torquing it down. “Go on, they don’t wait.”

She doesn’t hug me. She stands at the bottom of the ramp with her hands in her belt rag and watches me go up it, and when the ramp comes up she’s still standing in the same spot, and then the hull is between us and that’s the end of that.

The bench seating runs both walls and the webbing overhead is rated for crates, not bags. Nobody’s talking for the first hour. The lifter climbs through the haze layer and out of it, and the light coming through the port slots goes hard and white all at once, and a girl across from me flinches at it, which makes two of us.

Second hour, the kid beside me decides he’s had enough of the quiet.

“What’s yours?”

“What?”

“Your number.” He says it like I’m being slow on purpose. He’s bigger than me, wide through the shoulders in the way that means he grew early rather than that he worked for it. “Enrollment age. Come on.”

“Twelve, two hundred seven days, four hours, four minutes.”

He whistles through his teeth. “Twelve. You’re a baby.”

“You asked.”

“Fourteen and change. Fourteen, sixty-one days, eleven hours, nineteen minutes.” He sits back against the hull like he’s just laid a good hand on the table. “Sixty-one days. My mother wrote it down wrong the first time and we had to go back and get it corrected off the clinic record, which cost us, so I say the corrected one.”

The girl across from us gives her own without being asked, thirteen and a bit, and then the boy next to her, and it goes down the whole bench like that, everyone offering up the exact minute their body decided they were done being children, because it’s the only fact any of us has that isn’t identical to everyone else’s. Nobody asks where anyone’s from. Nobody has to. We’re all wearing the same grey and we all came out of the same kind of street, and the transport didn’t stop anywhere on the way here that looked different from the place it picked me up.

Somebody says he’s heard the first year is all reading. Somebody else says that can’t be right, because who’d bother teaching a couple thousand of us to read in one go, and the first kid says he heard it’s not one go, they run it fresh every time a new batch of lifters comes in, and nobody has an answer for that, so the conversation dies there and stays dead for the rest of the flight.

I sleep through the last hour without meaning to. What wakes me is the engine note dropping, and the shift of eight bodies all leaning the same direction at once.

The academy doesn’t look like a school. It looks like a freight yard that kept growing until it needed a wall around it, long low blocks in poured grey, roofs flat and unpainted, everything set out in lines that run past the edge of what I can see from the ramp. The ground under the boots is compacted grit that’s been rolled flat so many times it rings a little when a heel comes down.

There’s a mech in the near yard.

They’re washing it. That’s the first fact about it, before the size lands, before anything else, four people on a gantry with hoses working the shoulder plating down, water sheeting off the hull and going brown by the time it hits the drain grate. One of them has a scraper on a pole and is working at a seam behind the neck, hauling something out of it in strips, and the strips come out dark and rubbery and hit the ground with weight to them.

I know what I’m looking at. That’s a wash-down. My mother’s done a thousand of them at a quarter of that scale and I’ve held the light for maybe two hundred.

Then the person on the shoulder stands up straight to stretch their back, and my sense of the scale goes out from under me all at once, because they don’t come up past the collar plate. They’re a mark on it. The hull runs on past them and up and the head sits above that with its optics dark, and the shadow it lays down across the yard reaches most of the way to the transport I just came off.

I don’t know what I expected. On the public channels they film them from far enough out that two mechs fit in one frame, and the color goes flat, and they look like models being pushed around a table. This one has streaks down the flank where the water’s cut through the grime and hasn’t reached the rest. It has a repair plate riveted over the left hip in a slightly wrong grey. Somebody stood on a gantry and put that plate there and didn’t have the matching paint, and did it anyway, and it’s been like that for a while.

I want to see the seam behind the neck. Not the mech, the seam. I want to know what packs into it that badly and whether it’s the same problem every wash or a bad week.

I don’t say any of that out loud. There are two thousand of us in this yard, off a hundred transports out of a hundred streets that all look like mine, and every single one of them is staring at the same machine with their mouth slightly open, and I’d rather not be the one who says the wrong sentence in front of all of them on the first morning.

The intake hall is the only building here somebody bothered to make big on purpose. Bare pillars, ceiling three floors up, two thousand kids standing in it and still not filling it. The far wall has names cut into the stone, running the whole length of the hall in a band at head height and not stopping where the wall stops, carrying on around the corner into a corridor I can’t see the end of.

I can’t read them. I look at them for a while anyway.

The line moves faster than I expect. At the front there’s a woman with a tablet and a stylus and the flat voice of somebody who has said the identical eleven words several hundred times since dawn.

“Name.”

“Calder.”

She doesn’t look up. She turns the tablet around and holds the stylus out and says, “Mark here,” and taps one spot on the screen with her nail.

There’s writing on it. Four lines of it, and a box. I have no idea what any of it says. I take the stylus and I put a mark in the box she tapped, because that’s where her nail was, and I hand it back, and she’s already turning it around and swiping it away, and it occurs to me halfway through her next sentence that I’ve just agreed to something I couldn’t read and that nobody in this line is going to read theirs either.

“Dormitory four, bunk assignment’s on the door.” A pause, the length of a breath she’s taken several hundred times already. “Rune literacy, zero-six tomorrow. Don’t be late on a first day, they keep that one.”

“What’s rune literacy?”

She looks up. It’s the first time she’s looked up, and I understand immediately that she’s looking at me because I’m the one who asked, and that she’s not going to answer it either way.

“Zero-six,” she says. “Dormitory four.”

Dormitory four is eleven minutes’ walk out past the near blocks, and about halfway there the ground goes through me.

Not a sound first. A pressure, coming up through the new soles into the bones of both feet, three beats of it, then a fourth, spaced out slow. Somewhere off past the buildings, something the size of what they were hosing down in the yard has put a foot on the ground four times. Every kid on the path stops walking at the same moment, and then nobody says anything about it, and we all start again.

My room has a card slotted in a holder at eye height with a shape on it I can’t read.

Inside there are two bunks. The left one already has a bag on it, dumped rather than set down, one boot lace hanging off the side and a half-open toolkit sitting on the blanket, which is a kit nobody at this academy has earned yet on day one. No sign of whoever owns it. I stand there for a second longer than I need to, and then I take the right bunk, because I don’t especially want to have a conversation about which one is whose.

There’s a drawer built into the frame at the head end. It has a door on it.

I take the socket out of my pocket and turn it over once in my fingers, the way I have watched my mother do a hundred times without ever asking her what she was doing it for, and I put it in the drawer and push the drawer shut.

Then I sit on the bunk in the grey light coming off the yard lamps with my bag still packed at my feet, and outside the window two thousand of us are being walked in lines from one grey building to another grey building, and the floor comes up through my boots again, twice, farther off this time.
