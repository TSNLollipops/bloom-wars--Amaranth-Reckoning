# Qiraki Emotion Craft Reference

## Purpose

A working reference for writing emotion in this project without falling back on the shorthand every draft reaches for by default. Not a rulebook, a checklist to run against a scene that feels flat or feels like it is telling instead of showing.

## Theoretical grounding: how emotion actually works

Lisa Feldman Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made* (2017), constructed-emotion theory. The short version, because it changes how you write the sentence: emotions are not universal biological reactions with fixed facial and physical signatures that get triggered and then read off the body. The brain constructs an emotion in the moment out of raw interoceptive signal (heart rate, gut, temperature, muscle tension) plus prior experience plus the concepts the person's culture handed them. The same physical state gets constructed as fear in one context and excitement in another.

What this means for the prose:

- **There is no universal "fear feels like X."** A character's fear should be built out of *that character's* body and *that character's* prior experience. Trav's fear after Book 2 is a different construction than Trav's fear in Book 1, because he has more priors.
- **The interoceptive signal is real and specific and worth writing.** Barrett's affect is two-dimensional, valence (pleasant/unpleasant) and arousal (activated/quiet). Those two axes are physically felt. Write the physical, let the reader construct the label.
- **Naming the emotion is the weakest available move**, because naming is what the brain does last, and it's the part that varies most between people. "I am afraid" carries less than the specific body state that got constructed as fear.
- **Prediction is the mechanism.** The brain runs on prediction error. A character braced for something that doesn't come has an emotional event, and it is not "relief," it's a prediction that failed to resolve, which reads on the body as something closer to being off-balance.

This is the same discipline as the rune-tech grounding: use the real theory as far as it carries, and it carries a long way here.

## Working vocabulary

**Affect** — the raw two-axis feeling (valence, arousal). Always present, not always an emotion.
**Interoception** — the body's sense of its own internal state. Where the physical detail comes from.
**Construction** — the moment raw affect plus context becomes a named emotion for the character.
**Prediction error** — the gap between what the body braced for and what happened. Very often the actual dramatic content of a beat.
**Granularity** — how finely a given character distinguishes their own emotional states. A character trait, and it varies. Trav's granularity is low early and improves across the academy years. Reqa's is high and always has been.

## How an entry gets added

An entry goes in the bank when a scene needed an emotional beat and the first three attempts were all shorthand. Write down what actually worked. Include the character, the situation, the physical construction, and what got left unsaid. Do not add an entry that is just a nice phrase, this is not a phrase bank, it's a record of constructions that carried weight in context.

## Banned phrases / overused tics

These are the defaults the draft reaches for. Each one is a place where the work didn't get done.

- heart pounding / heart hammering / heart in his throat
- stomach dropping / stomach lurching / knot in his stomach
- blood running cold
- breath catching / breath hitching
- a chill ran down his spine
- he felt a wave of [emotion]
- something twisted in his chest
- he didn't know what he was feeling
- tears he didn't know he was holding
- **"**he realized**"** as the load-bearing verb of an emotional beat
- any sentence whose job is to name the emotion the previous paragraph already showed

The heart and stomach ones are not banned because they're untrue, they're banned because they're the two organs every draft uses and there are a great many other places a body registers a state.

## Replacement patterns

**Instead of naming, locate.** Where in the body, specifically, and what is it doing that is different from a minute ago. Jaw, hands, the back of the neck, the temperature of the fingers, the way weight sits on the feet, whether the shoulders know where they are.

**Instead of the emotion, write the prediction.** What did he brace for. Write the brace, then write what arrived instead. The gap does the work.

**Instead of a reaction, write an action that is slightly wrong.** A character doing an ordinary task with one component off is a stronger emotional signal than a described feeling. He counts the same row of fasteners twice. He puts the tool back in the wrong slot and doesn't notice.

**Instead of interiority, write attention.** What the character notices, and what they stop noticing, is a construction of state. A character in high arousal has narrow attention and it should show in what the prose describes.

**Instead of a clean single emotion, write the mixed one.** Real construction is rarely one thing. Two incompatible states held at once, without the prose resolving which one wins, is closer to true and much harder to write badly.

**Let the body know before the character does.** The physical construction can be on the page a full paragraph before the character has a concept for it. This is not a trick, it's the actual sequence.

## Per-character emotional register notes

**Trav Calder.** Low granularity early, by design and by background. He has a small vocabulary for his own states and defaults to the nearest large label, usually anger or nothing. His body is far more articulate than his interiority, which means his POV should describe physical state in detail and then name it wrong, or not at all. This improves across the academy years and the improvement is a real arc, track it. He also has a specific tell under high arousal: he goes procedural. He starts running checklists. The reader learns to read that as fear long before Trav does.

**Reqa.** High granularity. She has words for states Trav doesn't know are separate states, and she uses them precisely, which reads as cold to characters who can't do that. Her emotional writing is the inverse of Trav's, the naming is accurate and the body is under control, and the crack shows in timing rather than in physical tells. When she is genuinely off, she answers a beat late.

**Denic.** Constructs everything as a social problem. His emotional register runs through other people, what a state would look like to the room, and this is not calculation, it's genuinely how his affect resolves. He can be in real distress and the only signal is that his read of the room goes wrong.
