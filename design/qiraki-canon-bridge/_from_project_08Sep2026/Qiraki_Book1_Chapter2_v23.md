# ENLIGHTENED: THE ACADEMY ERA, BOOK 1, "THE KING AND THE CLIMB"

## Chapter Two: The First Pattern

*Discipline is not punishment. Discipline is what makes the rest of this life survivable.*

*Standard phrasing, posted at the head of every dormitory corridor in the Circle*

The ring comes at five. My mother told me it would, and I'm already awake for it.

Not by much. Long enough to have worked out that the ceiling over this bunk sits a full arm's length further off than the one at home, and that whatever's running behind the wall at this hour runs smoother than any compressor on our strip ever did. One steady note, no knock in it, nothing loose anywhere in the housing. Somebody's paid to keep it that way.

The ring itself isn't loud. It comes up out of the floor and the bunk frame at the same time, three short pulses, and then the light in the room comes up on its own from nothing to full over about four seconds, which is slower than it needs to be.

Denic doesn't move.

I get down off the bunk and my boots are where I left them and one of the two sets I packed is folded on the shelf above the drawer now, pressed, creased down both legs, better than my mother ever got it to lie flat. I didn't leave it like that. Somebody's been through my bag since I got here yesterday. Denic's got the same stack on his own shelf, pressed the same way, sized to him.

I get dressed. The collar clasp takes me three tries.

"Is it on fire," Denic says into his pillow.

"No."

"Then it can wait." He rolls over, gets one eye open, considers the room, and shuts it again. "My sister said the first ring's a warning ring and the second one's the real one."

"Was there a second one?"

A pause about two seconds long.

"There was not." He's up and off the bunk in one motion, already reaching for his own grey with the other hand. "She lied to me. On day one. I'm going to make her pay for that."

He gets dressed faster than I did and worse, collar half turned under itself at the back, and I don't say anything about it because I've got no idea yet whether you say that to someone you've known since yesterday.

The corridor's already moving when we get into it. Doors letting out one and two at a time down the whole length of it, everyone in the same grey, nobody talking much. A tall kid with a ridged crest down the back of his skull has to duck the hatch frame at the corridor's end without breaking stride, which means he's already learned where it is.

Denic looks at the back of his head as we follow him through.

"How do you learn that in one day."

"He probably hit it."

"Right." He seems satisfied with this. "Good. Same as everyone then."

The mess hall's fuller than I expected for this hour. Not the crush from last night, but not thin either, a couple thousand kids working through it in shifts and most of the shift already in. The noise sits at a different pitch than it did at dinner, lower, fewer voices going at once, a lot of chewing and not much else.

The line hands me a tray with three compartments filled and I recognize only one of them.

Denic takes us to a table with four already at it and drops in without asking, same as last night. Two of them are Hiopi. Their side of the bench folds out from under the table edge instead of up off the floor, set low enough for four legs to reach it, and it puts them at exactly the same eye line as the rest of us. Neither of them looks up.

"Morning," Denic says to the table generally.

One of the Hiopi kids says something back in a register that comes out lower than I expected and doesn't turn into words I know. The other keeps eating.

"That's fair," Denic says.

I get through most of the tray before a chime cuts through the hall, two notes, up then down, the kind you'd hear called on a transit line back home. The room drops out from under it in a wave anyway, conversation breaking in a band that travels back across the hall, and then a voice comes down out of the ceiling loud enough that nobody has to work at hearing it.

"First-year intake. Rune-patterning, zero-six hundred. Your instruction space is in your own dormitory, sublevel one. First corridor. First door on the right. Repeating. Rune-patterning, zero-six hundred, your own dormitory, sublevel one, first corridor, first door on the right."

It says it a third time after that. Slower.

Denic has his head tipped back at the ceiling like he expects to find something up there to look at.

"They could just put it on a board."

"No they couldn't."

He works that through and gets there about a second later, and whatever lands on his face he covers by going back to his tray. "Right," he says. "No. They couldn't."

Nobody at the table says anything else about it. Two tables over somebody's already asking somebody else which corridor is the first corridor, out loud, twice.

Sublevel one is one flight down off the dorm's own stairwell and the air changes on the way, cooler and drier, the grit underfoot going away to bare poured floor. First corridor. First door on the right.

The room past it is bigger than the dormitory block above it has any business hiding.

Desks in rows, and I stop counting at somewhere over a hundred and there's a lot more than that, going back far enough that the kids at the rear wall are small. Two hundred at least. The ceiling's low for the floor space, which makes it feel wider than it is, and there's no front to the room except that everyone's facing the same way.

Down there at the end, standing where a teacher would stand, there's a machine.

It's about my height. Built plain, no face on it, a smooth head section with a band of dark glass running most of the way around, and a body that doesn't pretend to be shaped like a person past having two arms and standing up. It's been standing there doing nothing as we walk to our chosen seat.

Denic takes a desk about two-thirds back and I take the one next to it. There's a flat grey box sitting square in the corner of each desk, unmarked, about the size of a deck of cards, lid open, empty. Neither of us touches it. The room fills up around us over the next few minutes.

The girl who ends up at the desk on my other side is small enough that the chair looks wrong under her, and she sits down and puts both hands flat on the desk and then doesn't move again. Not fidgeting. Not looking around. Just sitting there with her eyes forward, completely still, in a room with two hundred twelve-year-olds in it.

At zero-six exactly the machine starts walking the rows.

It carries a tray with it. On the tray there's a stack of bands, thin, grey, open at one end, and it hands one to every cadet it passes and doesn't stop moving to do it. It gets through the whole room faster than two hundred should take.

"This is your holoband," it says while it works. Flat voice, no volume behind it, and it carries to the back of the room anyway. "It is issued to you once. Replacement is possible. Replacement is slow, and the time it takes will come out of your instruction, not out of anyone else's."

It puts one in the box without breaking stride.

"Do not lose it."

The band's lighter than it looks. Denic has his on before I've picked mine up, upside down, and takes it off and turns it over and gets it on right the second time.

"Place it on either wrist," the machine says from three rows down. "It will fit."

It does. It closes to the size of my wrist on its own, not tight, and sits there weighing about as much as a card.

Then something says my name inside my head.

Not out loud. There's no sound in the room that wasn't already in it. It arrives in the same place a sound would arrive and skips everything in front of that, and it says *Trav Calder* in a voice with nothing much to it, and I have both hands on the edge of the desk before I've decided to.

Somebody four rows up makes a noise. Somebody else laughs at it. Half the room's got a hand up at one ear.

"That's rude," Denic says, out loud, to nobody. "That is a rude way to do that."

The voice keeps going. It tells me it's mine, that it works when the band's on and doesn't when it isn't, and then a small circle lights up in the corner of my eye and wipes out across the air over my desk.

There's a shape hanging in it about a forearm's length in front of my face. The academy, all of it, small enough to fit between my hands. Blocks and yards and the long low sheds, the wall around the whole of it, the intake hall, the yard with the mech in it, drawn in light and turning slowly. My dormitory picks itself out brighter than the rest of it. A line runs from the dormitory out through the yard and stops at a building I haven't been to. The voice reads the line off for me.

*Combat instruction. Friday. 0700.*

I look at it a while. There's a lot of academy beyond the part I've walked.

"Are you seeing this," Denic says.

"Yeah."

"Mine's got a line on it."

"Everyone's got a line on it."

"Right, but mine's *got* a line on it."

The girl on my other side has hers up in front of her and she's turning it with one finger, slowly, all the way around, and then back the other way, and she's already stopped looking at the parts of it that light up and started looking at the parts that don't.

The mini map shrinks back down to a small circle in the corner of my eye and the room goes quiet without anyone asking it to.

"Rune-patterning," the machine says. "Session one."

The space in front of my desk fills again, and this time there are two of them. On the left, hanging still, a shape in light. To the right of it, nothing, an empty volume about the size of the shape, waiting.

The shape on the left is a single straight line. Top to bottom, no curve in it, no branch. The plainest mark anything could make.

"On the left, the pattern. On the right, your own. Trace what you see. Follow the light."

A point brightens at the top of my empty space, and starts to move down.

I put my finger in it.

And I already know it.

I know it before my hand's gone a third of the way down, because I've made this exact mark a few hundred times on a board of nine wooden pegs that lived by the door of our flat for as long as I can remember having hands, molded plastic, one corner gone soft where it got left too near the stove one winter. Top-left peg to bottom-left peg. Straight down. You open with it. Every game starts with it and you can't do anything until you've laid it down, and if you're playing somebody younger than you you let them have it first because it's the only move in the whole game that nobody can lose with.

My finger gets to the bottom of the line.

The sound arrives inside my head the same way my name did, and it's not a note. It's a sound my own mouth already knows how to make, one long low vowel, and it stops clean the second my finger does.

I sit there.

Down the row somebody says *oh* out loud. Somebody behind me is already saying "wait, that's the," and doesn't finish it, and doesn't need to, because a second later there's a noise going through the whole room in a wave, the same shape of noise the mess hall made, two hundred kids getting there one after another, every one of them having walked in with it already in their hands.

Denic has both hands flat on his desk and he's staring at the space in front of him.

"That's the peg game."

"Yeah."

"That's the *peg game*." He turns all the way around in his seat to look at the room, and finds two hundred faces doing what his is doing. "Everybody had one of those."

"Yeah."

He turns back. He traces it again without being asked, watches the light do it, and then a third time.

"Right." He says it a lot quieter than everything else he's said today. "Sure."

"This is the First Pattern," the machine says.

"Cooperation is permitted and encouraged. Assistance is not the same as substitution. Your band will not advance on a trace it did not watch your own hand make."

Somebody tries it anyway about four rows up, two kids and one desk, and I hear one of them say "no, it just stopped" and the other one laugh at him.

We run the First Pattern for the rest of the period. Just the one. Two hundred kids drawing a straight line down a piece of air, over and over, until the shape stops being a shape you copy and starts being a shape your hand goes to.

Somewhere in the back half of it a kid stands up.

"Where's the teacher?"

The machine turns its head section around without turning its body.

"I am the instruction system."

"No, but where's the actual teacher."

"Instructor Halvorsen is observing this session through my optics." A pause that isn't waiting for anything. "As she is observing the other nine."

The kid sits down.

Nobody says anything for a couple of seconds, and I hear how quiet two hundred people can get, and I trace the line again because my hand's already going.

"So she's watching us right now," Denic says, at normal volume, to the whole room, "through the robot's *eyes*, which means she's seen my collar."

Two rows up somebody snorts.

"It's been like that since the corridor," Denic says. "It's been like that in front of the teach for forty minutes."

The girl on my other side gets up without a word, crosses behind our desks, and takes hold of the back of Denic's collar. She turns it back down where it belongs with two fingers and goes back to her own seat. She doesn't look at him while she does it, and by the time she sits back down she's already looking at something else.

Denic looks at me.

"Who is that."

"I don't know."

Fifteen minutes between sessions and the corridor takes all of us at once.

Everybody's got their band up. The mini map's out over half the corridor, two hundred copies of the same academy hanging in the air at different heights, kids walking through each other's, one kid down the far end using his to work out where the mess hall is from here and getting it wrong out loud.

The wide kid finds us, skin deep brown, hair black and cut close, dark eyes already sizing us both up before his hand's all the way out.

"Corw."

"Trav."

"Denic. My collar's fixed now, don't bring it up."

"Thirteen years," Corw says. "Zero days, zero hours, zero minutes."

Denic looks at him.

"That's not a real number."

"It's my number."

"Nobody's number is *zeroes*."

"Mine is." He says it the way you'd say a street you live on. "Ask the clinic."

"I'm going to."

"You can't, it's four systems out." Corw's already looking past us at who else is in the corridor, and takes half a step to go, and doesn't. "You got it first time? The line?"

"Yeah."

"Everybody did." He says it without much in it. "First one's supposed to be easy."

"Yeah."

He goes. Denic watches him get about six strides down the corridor and stop to say the same three sentences to somebody else.

"I like him," Denic says. "That's going to be a problem."

He's got an answer for everything today. So does everybody else I've watched try it.

The small girl comes past us on her way back in, olive-skinned, dark blonde hair pulled back plain, and Denic gets in front of her, which takes some doing because she goes around the outside of everything.

"Hey. Thanks for the collar."

"It was bothering me," she says, brown eyes already past him toward the door.

"That's fair. What's your name?"

"Ilyen."

She's already going. Denic lets her.

"Ilyen," he says to me. "Noted."

Second session runs three patterns instead of one, and the room finds out what the first two hours were for.

The Second Pattern is the First turned a quarter over. A straight line across instead of down, middle-left peg to middle-right. In the game it's the one you lay when you've got nothing, the one that means you're holding what you've got and daring the other kid to come at it. My mother used to put it down and then sit back off the board with her arms crossed and wait, and it drove me out of my mind every time.

The Third's the first one with a corner in it. Top-left across to top-right, then down and back to bottom-left, two lines meeting at the top corner, a wide open check laid on its side. That's a scoring shape. That's the one you're building toward the whole game.

I get all three inside twenty minutes and so does most of the room.

Then the machine gives us the Fourth, and the Fourth is the same two lines as the Third.

Exactly the same. Same corners, same length, same shape sitting in the air on my left, nothing about it different from the one I made twenty minutes ago. The only thing that's changed is where the light starts. It starts at the bottom-left and runs up to the top-right and then across to the top-left, which is the Third Pattern walked backward.

My hand doesn't want to do it.

I mean that mechanically. The light's there, the path is the path, and I've traced the same two lines already this hour, and my finger gets about a third of the way up the first one and slows down on its own without being told to.

It's not a rule I know. Nobody ever said you couldn't run it backward. It's that in nine years of that board on the floor by our door I never once saw anybody do it, not my mother, not me, not any kid on our ring, and I couldn't tell you why not.

I look up. Denic's stopped too, halfway up the same line, with his whole face screwed into a knot.

"This is wrong."

"It's the same one."

"It's *not*." He tries it again and stops in the same place. "It's the same one and it's wrong. Why is it wrong."

Two rows up a kid says, to nobody, "you can't go up," and somebody else says "who says," and the first kid says "I don't know."

The machine doesn't explain it. It says, "This is the Fourth Pattern," and then it says, "Trace it," and then it stops talking.

I trace it. It takes me eleven tries to get my hand to run the whole way without slowing, and the sound that lands in my head when I finish it isn't the Third Pattern's sound. Same shape, different mouth.

It isn't close.

Lunch is twenty minutes and Denic spends most of it with his forehead on the table.

"My brain hurts."

"You traced four lines."

"I traced four lines *nine hundred times* and one of them was a lie." He lifts his head enough to get at his tray. "I want to be clear that I'm not complaining."

"You are."

"I'm establishing a baseline."

Third session isn't rune-patterning.

Different room. One floor up, a few buildings over, benches instead of desks and a long rack down the back wall with actual tools on it, most of them clean, some of them not. Half the number of cadets. The band brings up a shape over my bench that turns out to be a servo actuator with its housing gone transparent, and it turns slowly the same way the mini map did. I know the shape of it before I know the name for it, same as I knew the wash-down before I understood the scale of it. My mother's had three of these apart on our own floor. Never one this clean.

The machine at the front of this one is a different machine and it doesn't hand anything out.

"Engineering foundations. Two hours. You are assigned a matchset for the duration of your enrollment. You will be issued a shared workshop station in the fourth week. Until then you work at these benches."

It reads the pairings out. It takes a while. When it gets to mine it says a name I don't catch the first half of, and a girl on the far side of the room stands up with a look on her face like the day has personally done something to her.

She's older than me. Taller by a real margin, and not the kind of taller that a year fixes, fair-skinned, grey-blue eyes going to the actuator before they land on me. She comes over and puts her hands on the bench across from me and both her fingertips are stained black at the ends, ink and something heavier under it, in past the point where washing does anything.

"Reaka," she says. No gap before the correction, same speed, same weight. "Reqa." It comes out so smooth I know she's said it that exact way a lot of times. She doesn't explain it. She just puts it down and moves on.

"Trav."

"You did two runes this morning?"

"Yeah."

"So did I." She sits. She looks at the actuator turning over the bench between us, and the look she gives it is nothing like the look she gave the room on the way over. "Four hours of drawing lines in the air."

"Four lines."

"Too little, for too long." She pulls the shape closer with one finger and starts taking it apart with the other hand, in the air, layer off layer, and she's not waiting for the machine to tell her to. "It's going to be exactly the same on Wednesday."

There's a tool tucked behind her ear. I don't think she knows it's there.

I've been watching Denic do this all day. He does it to strangers, at breakfast, in a corridor, at a table full of people who don't speak his language, and it works about half the time and he doesn't seem to mind the half where it doesn't.

So I say, "The band won't trace for anybody else's hand. Kid two rows back found out."

She stops with the housing halfway off.

"Why would I want somebody else's hand."

"You wouldn't. That's the problem with it."

She looks at me. Not long. About a second longer than those words have any business earning, and I can't read what's in it, and then she goes back to the actuator and gets the housing the rest of the way off.

"Hold that side," she says.

I hold that side. The shape holds still where she leaves it, and she gets in under the collar with two fingers and finds the pin seat and turns the whole assembly around so I can see what she's looking at, and she does all of it faster than the machine at the front has got through explaining what an actuator is.

We get it apart and back together three times before the session ends. She does most of it. I hold what she tells me to hold, and she doesn't once say the word for what she's doing before she does it, and by the third time through I know where the pin seat is without being shown.

At the end of it she pulls the tool out from behind her ear, looks at it, and puts it in her pocket.

"See you tomorrow," she says.

Denic talks the whole way back to the dorm and most of the way through dinner.

He's got a theory about the Fourth Pattern that changes three times before we're through the mess line. He's got a complaint about the bench height in the engineering room. He's got a full account of his own matchset, a kid called Doyle who apparently arranged the tools on their bench by size before saying a single word to him, which Denic reports as though it were a crime committed against him personally and then immediately says is the best thing that's happened to him since he got here.

Corw's at our table for dinner and so is Ilyen, and neither of them showed up together or asked to sit down, they just ended up there. Corw talks over Denic. Denic talks louder. Ilyen eats and says about nine words the entire meal and two of them correct something Corw got wrong about the mini map.

By the time we're back in the room, Denic's face-down on his bunk with his boots still on.

"Say something to me about tomorrow," he says into the blanket.

"It's the same day again."

"That's not comforting."

"You asked for something, not for comfort."

He makes a noise. Gets one boot off with the other foot and gives up on the second one.

"Four lines," he says. "Two hundred of us. And every single one of us had that toy."

He's asleep before I've got an answer for that, which is fine, because I don't have one.

The drawer at the head of my bunk has a door on it. I open it and the socket's where I put it, on its side, worn bright on the one flat. I turn it over once and put it back and push the drawer shut.

The band comes off my wrist and the mini map goes with it, and the room's just the room. Somewhere off past the wall the thing that runs at this hour is still running, one steady note, nothing loose in it anywhere.

I put my finger in the air over the bunk and trace the First Pattern once, straight down, in the dark, where nothing's watching it.
