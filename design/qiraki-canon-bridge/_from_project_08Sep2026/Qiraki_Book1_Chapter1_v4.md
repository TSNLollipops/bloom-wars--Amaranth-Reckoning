# ENLIGHTENED: THE ACADEMY ERA, BOOK 1, "THE KING AND THE CLIMB"

## Chapter One: Arrival

The drum wakes before I do. Four rings down somebody's already running a compressor and the whole shaft carries it up into the bones of my cot, the way it carries an argument two floors over, or the recycler kicking on at the wrong hour. I'm awake a good ten seconds before I remember why this particular morning is different from every other one that compressor's ever dragged me out of.

Then I remember why. Today's the day I leave for the Circle.

The ceiling over my cot sits close enough that I could touch it without straightening my arm all the way, a fat bead of condensation along the seam where our ring bolts to the one above it, the seam my mother's re-sealed twice already this winter and given up sealing a third time. I lie there and look at it and don't get up. Then I hear the kettle, and that decides it for me.

She's got her back to the door when I come out, sleeves shoved up past both elbows at an hour when there's no work yet to justify it. Same forward-set weight I carry standing there, weight riding toward the balls of her feet the way mine does, knees soft where mine stay locked tight, like the readiness never left her but whatever used to spring off it wore out somewhere along the way.

Hair in dreads, scraped back tight enough to show more gray at the roots than it used to. Skin a shade darker than mine, burns along both forearms catching the light off the one working panel over the stove, old ones, healed pale, never once covered by anything she wears. Dark brown cargo pants gone soft at the knees, a long-sleeve shirt faded down to charcoal from however many washes, both chosen for how long they'd last rather than how they'd look on her. Hands that'll end up more scarred than mine ever will, if I stay in this trade half as long as she has.

On the floor by her feet, half-open, sits somebody else's job, a courier drone with its undercarriage stripped bare and a fault light still blinking amber where she left it last night to come check on me instead of finishing it. Amber means a bad connector, not a dead motor, the kind of fault that looks expensive right up until somebody actually opens the housing and finds one scorched pin instead of a burned-out winding. She'll have it running again in under an hour once she's back to it. I know that from years of standing close enough to watch.

"There's egg," she says, not turning around. "Sit down."

"I'm not hungry."

"I didn't ask you that." A beat, then, quieter. "Eat it anyway. Last one I'm making you for a while."

So I sit, and I eat the egg. Same way she always does it, hard at the edges and soft everywhere else, too much pepper because she's never once measured it in her life. I eat all of it, even the burnt lace of white stuck to the pan, which most mornings I don't bother scraping loose.

The flat is one ring segment, four small rooms bent around the shared curve of the drum's own wall, and I can see three of the four from exactly where I'm sitting, because that's what a flat this size gives you, all of it at once whether you want it or not. The panel over the stove she never got the parts to replace properly, patched instead with a strip of the same grey sheet the repair strip downstairs sells by the meter. The tool wall, every wrench hung by size because she can't stand a drawer she has to dig through, because I've been the one hanging them back in order since I was tall enough to reach the low end of the rack without a step.

The couch with the burn in one arm from a soldering iron neither of us has ever admitted to setting down there. Six years of my life, the only years I actually remember, fits into about four seconds of just looking at it.

She turns around with her own mug and catches me at it.

"Get it all memorized?" Dry, not really asking.

"Most of it."

"Remember it well." Softer than the rest of it, eyes on the mug instead of me for a second. "We won't get many chances to see much of each other for a while." Then she sets it down, and her voice is back to ordinary before the mug's even out of her hand. "You've got the list."

Not a question.

"Packed off it twice, actually. Made sure."

"Show me anyway."

I open the bag on the floor and she goes through it standing over me, not touching, just moving her eyes down what's there the way she goes over a rig before she signs her mark to it. Two sets of the grey they sent over, folded worse than she'd have folded them. The soft cap. The wash kit. The card with my name on it that I can't read myself, in its sleeve, cord long enough to go around a neck. That's the whole of it. They were specific that it's the whole of it.

"You'll have less than half of them," she says. "Evens out faster than you'd think, not having more."

"Okay."

"It does." She says it a second time like the first one didn't land, and lets it go at that.

The bag's zip catches a hand's width from closed, right where it always does, and I have to walk it back and start the last stretch over before it'll run clean. It does this every single time and neither of us has ever once talked about fixing it.

She ties her own roll shut before we go, strap wrapped twice and knotted off with a scrap of fabric gone soft and grey with handling.

We're through the interior hatch between the front room and the hall before I've got my jacket all the way on, second sleeve still caught behind my back, her half a step ahead of me the way she always walks a doorway first. Rated to hold two different climates on either side of it, same as every hatch in every drum on this strip, not that either of us has ever needed it to.

At the flat's own door, the one with the pressure release, she stops with one hand already on it and doesn't push yet. Her eyes go once to the window, then back to the hatch behind us, quick, before she pushes it open and we go.

Eleven rings down, because the lift's been out since before my mother was even discharged, spiraling around the drum's central shaft past doors just like ours, stacked drum on drum on drum the whole way to ground, laundry strung between two rings on the level below ours, panels patched in three different greys where three different repairs happened three different years apart, until the stairwell dumps us out into the strip.

Six repair shops between our stack and the transit stand, shutters down but the smell of solder and cutting oil still sitting heavy over the street, the kind of smell that doesn't leave a block just because the doors are closed for the night. Sheet-metal awnings hang low enough that my mother ducks the third one without looking up, an overhang that's actually only low for about two strides of it, a duck she's been doing at that same spot since before I was tall enough to need to.

A Hiopi's coming the other way with a hand cart loaded past its own height, wrapped in some kind of draped fabric dyed a color bright enough to actually stand out against all this grey, four legs eating the ground fast enough that the crowd splits around him without anyone deciding to. I split with it too, half a step wide and back in behind my mother before the gap closes again. He doesn't slow down for any of it, doesn't need to, a body built for momentum doesn't stop being one just because the street's crowded.

An Osnius runs the stall three doors down, fur gone the color of wet rust where the morning light gets under the awning, eyes catching it back out a second later the way nothing on a human face ever does.

Above the gap where our drum leans in close enough to the next one over that you could throw something across it, a transport crosses low and slow, running lights strobing red-white-red along a hull. I know the profile from a hundred mornings just like this one, a hauler built long and flat-bellied for people instead of cargo, what everyone on the strip just calls a runner. Not cargo, not at that shape, not banking that tight this low over civilian rings. Troop movement, or a med run. Nobody on the strip looks up at it. I don't either, not really.

Past the end of the strip the ground drops away into the rest of the district, drum after drum going up out of the haze in every direction, none of them matched to any other, patched and re-patched in whatever grey happened to be cheapest the year a given ring needed it, laundry lines strung between towers that lean close enough to share them, a market two blocks over already lighting its stalls though nobody's buying yet.

Somewhere further out, past all of it, there's supposed to be open ground, actual sky without a haze layer sitting under it, but I've never once walked far enough to see where the drums stop and it starts.

One of the shop owners I like is out early enough to catch us going by, shutter half up already, tools laid out on the bench before there's light enough to use them by. He lifts his chin at my mother, the same particular half-inch he always gives her.

"Yssa."

She nods back the same half-inch.

He looks past her at me, and whatever's on my face must say more than I meant it to, because he huffs something short, the closest he gets to a laugh this early. "Don't let them make you mean, Trav. Come back and fix my compressor when you're a big shot pilot, you hear?"

"It's not broken."

"Give it time."

The public board outside the transit stand runs its slow scroll, a running column of the district's dead, name and unit and the ring they shipped out from, updated every time a transport brings word back. I can't read a single character of it any more than I can read the card in my own bag. Everyone on this strip knows what it's for anyway. Nobody stops walking to read it. Neither do we.

The transit stand's own support post has one of the Circle's seals stamped into the metal, a shape I've seen often enough to recognize without anybody ever having told me what it says, the kind of official mark you learn to read as a shape long before you learn to read anything else.

Twenty some kids are already standing near the ramp doing the same work with their faces that I'm apparently doing with mine, families around most of them going through some version of these last few minutes. One kid's got a woman's arm locked around his shoulders hard enough that I look away from it on reflex.

My mother stops about ten strides short of the ramp, which is as far as she's coming.

"Here." She's got it in my palm before I've even seen her hand move, small enough that my fingers close around it on instinct before I know what it is. "Don't lose that."

I open my hand. It's a socket, the smallest one out of her whole set, a size that's never once fit anything I've watched her put it on. Worn bright on one flat where a hand's held onto it a lot longer than any actual job ever called for.

"What's it for?"

"Nothing yet." She's already stepping back, already wearing the look she gets when a job's closed out and she's moving to the next one before she lets herself feel finished with the last. "Put it somewhere with a door on it. Not the bag."

"That's not an answer."

"No." She gives me something that's almost a smile, eyes bright in a way that doesn't have anything to do with the light out here. Gone as quick as it showed up. She reaches up and flattens my collar with two fingers, quick, the same motion she'd use squaring a plate before she torques it down. Her eyes cut to the ramp behind me, then back. "Go on. They don't wait on anybody."

I don't move yet, and she catches that the way she catches everything.

"I'll be right here. Same drum, same door." A beat. "Don't go soft on me up there. Don't go stupid either. There's a difference and I've watched you not know it before."

"I know the difference."

"We'll see." She looks at me a beat longer than the sentence needs, as much as I'm getting out of her this morning. "Go on, Trav."

My weight's already gone forward onto the balls of my feet before I've taken the first step up, the same way it goes before a bell I haven't heard yet, and by the time I look back from the top of the ramp she's already turned most of the way around, already halfway back into the rest of her morning. She doesn't hug me. She stands there with both hands back in her belt rag and watches me go, and then the hull comes between us.

Bench seating runs both walls, cargo webbing overhead rated for crates and not for the bags underneath it, the hull scuffed matte grey-green from hauling a hundred loads that weren't people, registry number stenciled small near the ramp, more maintenance panels than actual windows.

A Hiopi kid two seats down has his legs folded up wrong for a bench built for two points of contact instead of four, and doesn't complain about it once. Nobody talks for the first while. The lifter climbs through the haze layer and my ears do something unpleasant and then pop clear all at once, and the light through the port slots goes hard and white where it was grey a minute ago.

A kid down the bench decides somewhere past the haze that he's had enough of the quiet.

"What's your number?"

"What?"

"Enrollment age. Come on, it's tradition." Built wide through the shoulders in the way that means he grew into it early rather than earned it. "Mine's fourteen, sixty-one days, eleven hours, nineteen minutes. My mom wrote it down wrong and we had to fight the clinic for it."

"Twelve. Two hundred seven days, four hours, four minutes."

He whistles low. "Twelve. You're a baby."

"You asked!"

It goes down the bench after that, every kid handing over the exact minute their body decided it was done being a kid, because it's the one fact any of us has that isn't identical to the kid sitting next to him. Nobody asks where anyone's from. Nobody has to.

Somewhere past the haze the kid who started the number game points out we're supposed to be in uniform before we land, and there's no curtain on a transport built for hauling crates, so it turns into a group effort instead of anyone bothering to be private about it. A girl two seats down can't get her collar clasp to sit right and lets the kid next to her fix it without either of them saying much about it. I end up lacing another kid's boots because his hands are shaking too hard to manage it himself, and neither of us mentions that either.

We're all wearing the same grey by the time the lifter starts its final approach, and the transport didn't stop anywhere between my strip and here that looked any different from the block it picked me up on.

I sleep through the last hour without meaning to, and what wakes me is the engine note dropping and six bodies leaning the same direction at once.

The academy doesn't look like a school. It looks like a freight yard that kept growing until somebody put a wall around it, long low blocks in poured grey, roofs flat, nothing built to be looked at, only to hold weight and shed weather. The ground under my new boots is compacted grit rolled so many times it rings faintly under a heel.

There's a mech in the near yard, set up close enough to the intake path that every transport today's walked straight past it, and the first fact I actually register about it isn't the size. It's that they're washing it.

Two cadets on a gantry, older than anyone I've seen since we got off the transport, built into their size in a way that means they've been here a while. Hoses working the shoulder plating, water sheeting off the hull and running brown by the time it hits the drain grate below. One of them's got a scraper on a pole, working a seam behind the neck, hauling something out of it in dark rubbery strips that hit the ground with real weight behind them. I know what I'm looking at before I understand the scale of it. That's a wash-down. My mother's done a thousand of them and I've held the light hundreds of times.

Then the one on the shoulder straightens up to stretch a back that's been bent too long, and the scale finally lands on me, because they don't come up past the collar plate. They're a scuff mark on it. Somewhere back far enough I can't fully trust the memory anymore, there's a cockpit with my hand on a yoke too big for it, my father's hand wrapped around mine so it wouldn't slide off. Whatever that was, it wasn't this.

The hull keeps going past them, up, and the head sits above all of that with its optics dark, and the shadow the whole hull throws across the yard reaches most of the way back to the transport I just came off. There's a repair plate riveted over one hip in a shade of grey that doesn't quite match the rest of the hull, somebody's patch job from a paint lot that ran out, left on anyway because leaving it on cost less than matching it.

It's the seam behind the neck I actually want a better look at. Not the machine around it. Just that seam, what's packed into it that needs scraping out this badly, and whether that's a bad week or every week.

I don't say any of it. A couple thousand of us are standing in this yard, off a hundred transports from strips that all look like mine, every mouth a little open at the same machine. I'd rather not be the one who says something wrong first.

The intake hall is the one building here somebody bothered making big on purpose, bare pillars, ceiling three floors up, two thousand kids standing in it and still not filling it up, more of them not human than I expected, fur and hide and one kid built taller and broader than anyone else in the hall. The far wall's got names cut into the stone in a band at head height, running the length of the hall and around a corner I can't see the end of.

I can't read them. I look anyway.

The line moves faster than I expect. At the front there's a woman with a tablet and the flat voice of somebody who's said the same eleven words a few hundred times since before dawn.

"Name."

"Trav Calder."

"Enrollment age."

"Twelve years, two hundred seven days, four hours, four minutes."

"Nobody needs the minutes, kid." Same flat voice as everything else, like she's said this exact line to the last fifty kids who made the same mistake. She turns the tablet around and holds the stylus out. "Mark here." Taps one spot with her nail.

There's writing on the screen, four lines of it and a box. I have no idea what it says. I put my mark in the box anyway, because that's where her nail was, and she's already turning it back around and swiping it clear before I've finished making it. I've just agreed to something I can't read. Same as every kid in this line.

"Dormitory four, bunk's on the door." A pause worn smooth from repeating it. "Rune-patterning, zero-six tomorrow. Don't be late your first day. They remember that one."

"What's rune-patterning?"

She looks up for the first time since I got to the front. Actually looks at me, not through me. "You'll find out at zero-six same as the other two thousand." Not unkind. Just done answering that question for the day. "Next."

Dormitory four turns out to be the better part of a walk past the near blocks, and somewhere around the halfway point the ground goes through me before the sound does. A pressure first, coming up through new boot soles into the bones of both feet, three beats of it and then a fourth, spaced slow. Off past the buildings, something roughly the size of what they were hosing down in the yard has put a foot on the ground four separate times. Every kid on the path stops walking at the exact same second, and then nobody says a word about it, and we all start again like it didn't happen.

The dormitory block itself starts behind its own climate hatch, same rating as the one at home, sized to hold whatever mix of species ends up rotated through a given wing. Nobody in the crowd funneling through it slows down for it either.

My door reads my card, and a second shape joins the one already sitting on the panel. No way to tell which mark belongs to which of us. The kid already inside when I get the door open is squinting at the card still in his own hand like it's said something to offend him personally, stocky and fair-skinned, hair a reddish brown gone flat on one side from the transport seat.

"You my bunkmate?"

"Yeah. Trav."

"Denic." He doesn't get up, just jerks his chin toward the far bunk without looking at it, already back to digging through a bag half emptied across his own. "That one's yours. I called this one first, so don't start anything, I don't have it in me for a bunk war on day one."

"Wasn't planning one."

"Smart, because I'd have won anyway. Everyone back home says I'm annoyingly hard to argue with." He hauls a toolkit out from under a spare set of boots, half-assembled, nicer than anything a first-year's got any right to yet, and sets it on the blanket like it's the only object in the whole bag worth protecting. "Don't ask where I got this. It's a long story and the version with the honest ending makes me look worse than the one I'm planning on telling you."

"Wasn't going to ask."

"Good, because I was going to tell you anyway and I haven't decided which version yet." Grins at me, quick, there and gone, green eyes right there with it. He flips the kit open, runs a thumb down the row like he's checking nothing walked off between the dorm door and here, and looks at me properly for the first time since I came in, a second grin already halfway there before whatever he's about to say has actually caught up to it. "You read?"

"No."

"Nobody does. Apparently that's the entire first lesson tomorrow, whole room illiterate together, and I already hate it and haven't even sat through it yet." He says it with the specific bitterness of somebody who's already decided exactly how much he plans on enjoying something. "You see it? The one on the gantry, they had it right on the intake path." He says it like there's only one mech that could possibly matter this evening.

"Yeah."

"Same." Whatever he's about to say next, he actually stops moving to say it, hand going still over the open kit for the first time since I walked in. "Did something to my stomach I've never felt it do before."

"Mine too."

"Good. If it hadn't, I'd have real concerns about you as a roommate." He shuts the kit, stands, stretches until a joint in his back pops loud enough to hear across the room. "Come on. If we don't eat now I'm told it gets worse later. Something about two thousand people and one serving line."

The mess hall is the first place since the transport that actually sounds like this many people in one space, noise I feel before I properly hear it, trays clattering, a couple thousand voices all doing something different at once. The serving line doesn't wait for anyone to decide what anything is before it lands on a tray, and I end up with three items I couldn't name and something I'm fairly sure used to be bread. The wide-shouldered kid from the transport bench passes two lines over, already deep in conversation with somebody else, gone into the crowd again before I've decided whether a nod's the right thing to do about it.

Denic drops into the first open seats he finds without checking who else is already at the table, three other kids already partway through their trays at the far end, nobody paying either of us any attention. Everyone else looks like they sorted into clusters somewhere between the gate and here that I apparently missed forming.

"First meal always looks like this," he says, like he's already decided that's a fact instead of a guess. "Give it a week."

"You've done this before?"

"No. Sister did, though, and I've decided to trust everything she told me except the part about the food getting better, which I refuse to believe until I taste it for myself." He keeps going for another minute, something about whether the tray compartments are sized wrong on purpose, and then lets it drop when I don't answer, doesn't push it, doesn't ask what's wrong either.

I eat without tasting much of it. The socket riding in my pocket. The seam behind the mech's neck. The names on the wall too far off to read even if I could read anything at all. Whether his first day looked anything like this one.

Around us the hall thins out in stages, whole sections emptying at once like something's called them elsewhere, until it's down to stragglers and the two of us, and neither one of us moves to leave first.

We walk back without talking much, the kind of quiet that isn't uncomfortable so much as spent, both of us out of anything left to say about a day that's already said more to us than either of us actually asked it to.

I sit down on the bunk that's mine now and take the socket out of my pocket where it's ridden the whole day, and turn it over once, the way I've watched her do it a hundred times, without ever asking what it was for. The drawer at the head of the bunk has a door on it, same as she told me to look for. I put the socket inside and push it shut.

Out past the window, the ground does the same thing it did on the path in, once, then again, fainter this time, farther off. I feel it through the bunk frame instead of my boots, one thin shiver up through the metal and gone. I'm mostly asleep before the second one finishes.
