## Design principle

Combat class is the one curriculum that’s been treated so far as pure narrative assertion, “measures raw pilot aptitude most directly” (Qiraki_Concept.md), with no theoretical spine under it the way rune-patterning has graph theory and engineering has real mechanical fields.

This document builds that spine. Real backbone for the whole seven years: Fitts and Posner’s three-stage model of motor skill acquisition, genuine, established motor-learning theory, cognitive stage (slow, effortful, consciously monitored), associative stage (refining, inconsistent, still effortful under pressure), autonomous stage (fast, fluid, requires little conscious attention). This maps almost exactly onto the shape already locked elsewhere in the project without having been designed for it: Years 1-2’s non-mech, deliberately simplified kid-games sit naturally in the cognitive stage, Years 3-4’s, roughest, most effortful stretch is the associative stage doing exactly what associative-stage training looks like in real athletes, and Book 5’s already-locked “fluid and evasive” shift is, almost too neatly, the literal, textbook description of reaching the autonomous stage. Nothing here contradicts anything already locked, it retroactively explains why the existing shape already tracks real science this closely.

## Year-by-year curriculum, extrapolated

Year 1 — Physical literacy, cognitive stage. Real sports-science term, “physical literacy,” the foundational movement competencies (balance, coordination, spatial awareness, basic strength) that every later specialized skill gets built on top of, not sport-specific yet.

King of the Hill (already on the page, Book 1’s year-end exam) is exactly this kind of assessment, general athletic competence under mild competitive pressure, no weapon, no mech, no specialization, because none of that has anything to build on yet. Real, ordinary youth athletic-development content, not invented for the setting. The weekly class itself, distinct from the exam, is already full contact. Low-tier academy training mechs, wound-emulation active, from the very first Friday session. Real content stays genuinely physical-literacy-level, basic reactive drills, simple contact, nothing tactically sophisticated yet, but the stakes underneath the simplest drill are never simulated. A cadet’s very first taste of this world’s core mechanic happens here, not later, months before anyone’s framing it as brave or dangerous out loud.

Year 2 — Tactical fundamentals, still cognitive stage. Real content: basic spatial reasoning and small-unit tactical awareness, reading terrain, positioning relative to teammates and objective, still no weapon-specific skill. Capture the Flag (already locked, the week-long shifting-maze exam) is a genuine, real training format for exactly this, team objective-based movement through unpredictable terrain, still firmly cognitive-stage, cadets are still consciously thinking through every decision rather than reacting.

Year 3 — Specialization begins, entering associative stage. Real content: this is the year deliberate practice theory (Anders Ericsson’s research on how expertise actually gets built, focused, effortful repetition on a narrowed skill set, not broad general athleticism) starts applying directly, matching the already-locked class specialization lock. A Meeps cadet’s associative-stage reps look nothing like a Tank cadet’s, same underlying motor-learning stage, genuinely different content, mirroring the same split already established in both other curricula’s own Year 3 entries.

Year 4 — Associative stage under escalating stress. Real content: stress inoculation training, a genuine, named field within sports and military psychology (Donald Meichenbaum’s original framework, since adapted widely into combat-sport and tactical training), which escalates scenario complexity, opponent skill, and duration under pressure that was already real from Year 1, not a threshold where real consequence first enters the picture. This is the actual science behind why this year specifically is the physically roughest, already locked (Trav and Vrassik’s real, repeated injuries): the associative stage is inherently the least efficient, most error-prone stage of skill acquisition, cadets are clumsier and less consistent here than they’ll be by Year 5, and this is exactly the year the curriculum pushes scenario difficulty hardest against that clumsiness. The danger was never new, what’s new is asking bodies still in the least reliable stage of learning to do meaningfully harder things with it. Worth keeping visible as its own piece of the “quiet structural critique” discipline: a real, working training method, run with genuine appropriate escalation, aimed at children whose bodies have been absorbing real consequence since they were twelve, the method isn’t the problem, the population it’s applied to without exception is.

Year 5 — Automaticity emerging, entering the autonomous stage. Real content: this is where motor learning research says fluid, low-effort execution should start appearing, skills that no longer require full conscious attention to execute, freeing attention for higher-order tactical reading instead. Directly, almost exactly matches the already-locked Book 5 shift, Trav moving fluid and evasive rather than just surviving, this isn’t authorial flourish, it’s precisely what the autonomous stage looks like in a real athlete, arriving in exactly the year real motor-learning timelines would predict it for someone with four prior years of structured, escalating practice already behind them.

Year 6 — Motor schema transfer. Real content: motor schema theory (Richard Schmidt’s Generalized Motor Program research), the real finding that a skilled performer doesn’t relearn a new but related movement from scratch, they transfer an existing underlying schema onto new equipment or a new variation of a familiar action. Directly, doubly supports Trav’s already-locked equipment innovation this exact year (lance to sword, alongside the rune-tech and engineering curricula’s own Year 6 contributions already locked in Qiraki_Concept.md), a third, genuinely independent discipline converging on the same story beat: he isn’t learning to fight with a sword from zero, he’s transferring seven years of an established motor schema onto a new blade shape, which is exactly why the switch reads as a discovery rather than a fresh skill.

Year 7, Devereux-Kastel — Full scenario integration. Real content: force-on-force, live-scenario tactical training, the real, standard capstone format used across actual military and law-enforcement training pipelines once foundational skills are in place, unscripted, adversarial, closer to the genuine unpredictability of real combat than any drilled exercise before it. Matches the already-locked “by year 7, students are working weapon systems directly” and gives the RPD library built across six prior years its first genuinely open-ended test, directly setting up the pilot arc’s real combat immediately following graduation.

## Where real theory runs out, LOCKED boundary

Genetic and stimulant upgrades exceeding real human performance ceilings. Everything above, physical literacy, deliberate practice, stress inoculation, automaticity, motor schema transfer, force-on-force training, is genuine, well-established sports and military training science, no invention required, all real methods training real human bodies toward real, known biological limits. The break happens specifically at the points-shop genetic/stimulant line (Quickstep, Ironvein, Syncline, Hotwire, see Qiraki_Points_Shop_Catalog.md), because real human reaction time and neuromuscular response have actual, measured floors, no amount of real training pushes a human past them, that’s a hardware limit, not a skill ceiling. Everything the curriculum teaches is real training working within those limits.

The upgrades are what let a cadet exceed them, and that’s the genuine break point, the same category of invented-science line the other two curricula each draw explicitly, kept visibly, deliberately distinct here rather than blurred into “just really good training.”

### First Contact

Real field: physical literacy, applied with wound-emulation active

Taught: Year 1, the first combat class session Function: A cadet’s actual first experience of full-contact training, low-tier academy mech, simple reactive drills, real stakes from the opening session rather than eased into across the year.

Notes: Worth a real, specific on-page moment eventually, the gap between how gentle King of the Hill looks from outside and how real the very first Friday actually is, is exactly the kind of quiet, unstated contrast this project’s structural-critique discipline is built to carry. Arena mechanic, LOCKED, author’s own draft dialogue, first on-page version of this scene:

“Pick your weapon and face it,” says the instructor. “Face what?” “You will fight each other.”

Cadets pick a weapon (standard-issue G-tier, no personalization, per the existing points-shop lock), pair off, fight. An automated arena safety system reads combat vitals/damage in real time and pulls a cadet clear automatically the moment they’re about to cross a lethal threshold, restocked, healed, and returned to a return point (working term, “the Spawn Line,” author’s pick, open to change) where fresh weapons wait and they can go again. No instructor intervention needed, no manual extraction, the system does it alone.

Why this matters beyond the scene, LOCKED: this is the actual mechanical reason the academy-era casualty rate is as low as it’s already locked (~1% or less, see Qiraki_Weapons_And_Progression.md).

An automated system that pulls a cadet before the lethal line is a near-perfect safety net in a way the military era’s manual, Munti-dependent extraction under real enemy fire can never be. This isn’t just flavor for the training scene, it’s the concrete cause behind an abstract number, academy training is survivable specifically because the safety net doesn’t depend on anyone reaching you in time, the war is dangerous specifically because it does.

Staging note, per the locked instructor ratio: given roughly one human instructor per 2,000-cadet cohort, this has to read as a mass assembly, the instructor’s lines said to a packed group, not a private aside to Trav. The real weight lands once pairings are called and Trav realizes the instruction applies to him specifically, not before.

Craft note: worth keeping Trav’s actual first opponent unnamed, a stranger the story never returns to, reinforcing the “2,000 kids, faceless scale” contrast the project explicitly wants rather than pre-loading his first real fight with narrative meaning it doesn’t need yet.

Long-arc payoff, flagged for later books, not yet placed: the Spawn Line’s respawn-with-fresh-weapons structure is a real-stakes version of the exact game loop this generation grew up on, plausibly part of why twelve-year-olds can psychologically tolerate this at all, see the adolescent-psychology standard in Qiraki_Book_Expansion_Plan.md.

Worth a deliberate, quiet moment somewhere in the military-era books when that safety net simply isn’t there anymore, no automated pull, no Spawn Line, no infinite weapons, the exact comfort this scene establishes revealed as something that was never coming with them.

Session length and scoring, LOCKED: the session runs a full four hours, the Spawn Line’s fight-extract-heal-return loop repeating continuously across that window, meaning a single combat class generates many individual bouts per cadet, not one.

Each bout is a “success” in the already-locked Year 1 point-generation formula (see Qiraki_Weapons_And_Progression.md’s combat-class point column, 20 points/success at month 1, climbing 20/month), so the Spawn Line isn’t only safety infrastructure, it’s the literal scoring mechanism, cadets who perform best across the four hours rack up the most successes and walk away with meaningfully more points than cadets who don’t, real, immediate, legible stakes from the very first Friday. Cohort scale note: only aspiring pilots attend combat class, roughly 1,000 of each 2,000-strong monthly intake, see the pilot/mek split now locked in Qiraki_Concept.md’s cohort-scale section.

Real field: deliberate practice, path-specific specialization

Taught: Year 3

Function: The first genuinely path-differentiated combat coursework, a Meeps cadet’s close-range footwork drills look nothing like a Tank cadet’s positioning drills from this point forward.

Notes: Direct parallel to the class-choice debate chapter already locked for Book 2, this is the actual coursework that debate chapter was choosing between.

### Live Contact Sparring

Real field: stress inoculation training Taught: Year 4 Function: Full wound-emulation-active sparring, same real stakes cadets have carried since Year 1, now run at longer duration, against more skilled opponents, and inside deliberately harder scenarios.

Notes: The direct mechanism behind the already-locked Trav/Vrassik injury exchange across Years 3-4. Not the moment real danger enters their training, the moment real danger meets a body still in the associative stage being asked to do meaningfully more with it than Year 3 did. The training method itself is sound and genuinely escalates the way a real program should, the population it’s run on without exception is the actual, unstated problem, a clean example of the project’s standing “never state the critique outright” discipline.

### Flow Drills

Real field: automaticity, autonomous-stage motor learning

Taught: Year 5

Function: Reduced-cueing, high-repetition combat sequences specifically designed to push a skill out of conscious attention, freeing bandwidth for tactical awareness layered on top.

Notes: The concrete curriculum behind Book 5’s already-locked “fluid and evasive” shift, good candidate for an actual on-page training-montage-adjacent chapter rather than only showing the result in a mission.

### Transition Trials

Real field: motor schema transfer

Taught: Year 6

Function: Structured drills specifically testing whether an existing weapon skill transfers cleanly onto a new weapon shape, not teaching a new weapon from zero.

Notes: The actual coursework Trav would have been doing the same year he rigs his own sword-and-servo upgrade, worth a scene showing the formal drill right alongside his informal, self-driven tinkering, two versions of the same real phenomenon happening at once.
