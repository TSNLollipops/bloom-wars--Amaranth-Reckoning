**RUNE-PATTERNING**

*A Reader's Primer — the Alphabet, the Grammar, and the Peg Game*

Every cadet at the Academy learns to read and write on the same nine-peg board their family kept by the door when they were small, a cheap wooden square that decided who won and lost a schoolyard game generations before anyone used it to talk to a machine. This primer walks through what that board actually does: the alphabet hiding inside it, the grammar built on top of that, and the childhood game it grew out of, rebuilt here as something you can genuinely sit down and play.

# The Board

Nine pegs, a fixed 3×3 grid, numbered like a phone keypad:

| **7** | **8** | **9** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **4** | **5** | **6** |
| **1** | **2** | **3** |

 

**Trace — **a straight line drawn peg to peg. Direction matters as much as shape. The same two pegs traced in reverse order is a *different* instruction, not a mirror of the first.

**Knot — **three or more traces meeting at a single peg, the point where a pattern stops being a shape and starts being an operation.

**Circuit — **several traces, read in sequence, one after another.

**Circle — **a closed loop that feeds back into itself, the rare, advanced shape that runs without further input.

Two registers live on the same board. Plain, no-knot traces spell language, this is **rune-script**, a working phonetic alphabet. Anything with a knot or a closed loop is **rune-form**, the technical register that actually does something to a machine.

# The Alphabet (Rune-Script)

Twenty-one letters, five vowels and sixteen consonants, built entirely from straight, non-converging traces on the same nine-peg board.

**Vowels — **AH 7→1  •  EH 4→6  •  EE 9→3  •  OH 8→2  •  OO 1→3

**Voiced/voiceless pairs, same shape, opposite direction —**

T 7→9→1   /   D 1→9→7

S 7→3   /   Z 3→7

N 9→1   /   M 1→9

K 8→4   /   G 4→8

P 8→6   /   B 6→8

F 2→4   /   V 4→2

**Unpaired consonants — **W 1→3→9  •  L 3→9→7→1  •  R 5→9  •  H 5→1

A trace from peg 7 down to peg 1, alone, spells **AH**, the same shape used, unknowingly, in the very first pattern any cadet learns. A name like **TRAV** spells out as four short strokes, none of them touching, nothing converging, legible at a glance as writing rather than machine-code.

# The Peg Game

Before it's a curriculum, it's a toy. “Lines you traced with a finger to win or lose, depending on the pattern you built,” and nine shapes carry the whole folk tradition, each with its own schoolyard meaning:

**First **(7→1) — “start,” the opening move.

**Second **(4→6) — “hold,” a defensive move, you've got nothing, dare them to come at it.

**Third **(7→9→1) and **Fifth **(1→3→9) — “reach,” a scoring move, two different shapes, same meaning.

**Fourth **(1→9→7) — no folk meaning at all. This direction was never a legal move. It just feels wrong.

**Sixth **(2→8, 4→8, 6→8, a knot) — “block,” the game's one aggressive move, the one kids fight over.

**Seventh **(3→9→7→1) — “wall,” a stalling move.

**Eighth **(2→4, 2→6, 2→8, a knot) — “trap,” the other aggressive move, the natural answer to a block.

**Ninth **(a five-trace loop closing back on itself) — no folk meaning either. Nobody could actually play it. Five clean lines cross a real hand before it finishes tracing them.

That last detail, an unplayable pattern held back for last, is the seed of a real rule: nothing on a real board can cross itself.

# Play It Yourself

*What follows isn't canon. It's a working reconstruction, built to honor what the folk meanings above already imply about how the schoolyard game must have worked.*

**You need: **paper and two pens in different colors, or a corkboard with nine pins and two colors of string.

**Setup: **draw the 3×3 grid, dots numbered as above.

**Turns: **players alternate, one trace per turn, in their own color, with a small arrowhead marking direction. A trace joins any two pegs not already joined by a trace of either color. A trace may never cross a trace already on the board, exactly the limit that made the real Ninth Pattern unplayable.

**Reach (how to win): **complete an open, two-trace bend through three pegs, both traces your color, with nothing closing the shape into a third line, and call it. You win immediately.

**Hold: **once per game, instead of tracing, you may pass. This costs your opponent a turn's worth of patience while their crossing limits keep tightening and yours don't.

**Block and Trap: **land a third trace of your color on a single peg and you've tied a knot there. That peg locks, no one may draw through it again for the rest of the game. Do this on the peg your opponent needs and their Reach is dead for good.

**Wall: **three of your traces boxing off one full side of the board doesn't win by itself, but it can strand your opponent's pegs behind lines they can no longer cross to reach.

If the board fills up, every legal non-crossing trace drawn, and nobody has called Reach, whoever holds more knots wins the position. Equal knots, the board holds. Nobody loses. Which, if you've been paying attention to the rest of the story, is probably not an accident either.
