# THE QIRAKI FILES — BOOK ONE

## Chapter One

*ALPHA DRAFT — retained for reference only, not canonical. Superseded by the new full-scope Chapter One draft (2026-08-09), which opens with Trav’s pre-academy home life per Qiraki_Defect_Queue.md DEC-02. See DEF-09 for status history.*

The kettle’s going before I’m even properly awake, which tells me she’s been up longer than she’s saying. My mother doesn’t put the kettle on for herself. She drinks yesterday’s cold water out of habit, always has, and the only time the kettle goes on this early is when there’s a reason for it, and this morning the reason is me.

I come out into the front room still doing up the second boot and she’s got both mugs down already, steam coming off them, and she doesn’t look up from the mugs when she talks.

“Eat something before you go. Not just the bread.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“I didn’t ask if you were hungry.”

I eat the egg. It’s easier than the argument.

The flat’s small enough that I can see the whole of what I’m leaving from where I’m standing, the panel she never got around to replacing over the stove, the wrench set hanging by size on the wall because she can’t stand a drawer she has to dig through, the couch with the burn mark from a soldering iron neither of us has ever admitted to. Twelve years in these four rooms. It doesn’t look like much of anything, standing here trying to fix it in my head before I go.

She catches me looking and doesn’t say anything about it, which is its own kind of saying something.

“You’ve got everything,” she says. Not a question.

“I’ve got what they told me to bring.”

“Same thing, this once.” She wipes her hands on the rag that’s always tucked into her belt whether she’s working or not, an old habit from years I don’t remember, and comes around the counter, and for a second I think she’s going to hug me, and she doesn’t, she just puts both hands on my shoulders and looks at me the way she looks at a rig before she signs off on it. Checking for something. I don’t know what she finds.

“You’ll be fine,” she says, and it isn’t really to me.

She walks me as far as the transit stop and no further, which I understand without her explaining it, some things are easier done at a door than dragged out along a platform. The walk takes ten minutes through streets I could do with my eyes shut, the block’s whole strip of repair shops still shuttered this early except for the smell of solder that never quite leaves the air even overnight, sheet-metal awnings hanging low enough over the walkway that my mother ducks under the worst of them out of pure habit, same as she’s done my whole life. Overhead, past the gap where two buildings lean close enough to almost touch, a cargo hauler crosses low and slow against a sky that’s more haze than color this time of morning, the kind of ordinary sight I’ve never once looked twice at and probably won’t see again for years.

Nobody out this early waves at us by name, but two of the shop owners nod the particular nod that means they know exactly whose kid is leaving and don’t need to say anything about it. My mother nods back the same way. This is the whole neighborhood’s way of saying something without saying it, and I’ve had twelve years to learn how to read it even without being able to read anything else.

The transport’s already sitting there with its ramp down, half a dozen other kids my age standing around it in various states of trying not to look like they’re about to cry, parents doing the same thing with slightly better control of their faces.

“Here.” She presses something into my hand at the last second, small enough that my fingers close around it before I’ve even seen what it is. “Don’t lose that.”

I look down. It’s a socket, the smallest one from her set, the size that doesn’t fit anything I’ve ever seen her use it on. Worn smooth on one flat, the way metal gets when a hand’s held it a lot longer than the job ever called for.

“What’s it for?”

“Nothing yet.” She’s already stepping back, already got that look she gets when a job’s done and she’s moving to the next one before she lets herself feel finished with the last. “Put it somewhere you won’t lose it.”

I don’t get anything else out of her. She’s good at that, closing a conversation the way she closes a panel, quick, before you can see what’s still loose inside it.

The transport isn’t built for comfort. Bench seating runs down both walls, cargo webbing overhead for the packs nobody was told to bring anything bigger than, and the whole hull has the look of something meant to carry crates first and people second, gone over just enough to make the second use legal. Nobody’s talking much. A girl across from me has her uniform folded on her knees instead of worn, the standard-issue grey that came in the enrollment package, cut plain, no rank markings yet, nothing to distinguish one of us from the next except the boots, which come in exactly one size range and fit almost nobody exactly right. I’ve got mine on already. Most of the others haven’t bothered yet, like putting them on makes it more real than it already is.

The transport takes four hours and I sleep through most of it without meaning to, and when the noise changes, engines dropping pitch, bodies shifting around me toward the windows, I wake up already knowing we’re close before I’ve even opened my eyes.

The academy doesn’t look like a school. It looks like a city that got tired of pretending it wasn’t also a fortress, low grey buildings running out toward a horizon that’s mostly training fields, poured concrete and hard angles, nothing built to be looked at, only to hold weight and keep weather out. Past all of it, far enough that it takes a second to understand the scale of what I’m looking at, a training mech standing dormant in an open yard, arms hanging, head bowed like it’s asleep on its feet.

I’ve seen mechs before. Everyone has, on a screen, in the fights they show on the public channels, tiny figures made huge by distance and camera work, colors flattened out by whatever the broadcast does to them. This one isn’t flattened by anything. The hull’s a grey I don’t have a real word for, not the dead grey of the buildings around it, something closer to the color a knife goes right before it’s finished being sharpened, and where the joints show through at the shoulder and the knee there’s a darker band underneath, almost black, like the parts that actually move needed something the rest of it didn’t. Sunlight’s catching one whole side of it low and orange, this early in the morning, and the shadow it throws back across the yard is longer than the transport I just got off.

This is the first one I’ve ever seen close enough that the size actually means something in my chest instead of just my eyes. It’s not moving and it still looks like it could, and there’s a seam running down the center of its chest plate that doesn’t look like a weak point so much as a place something inside it breathes through, and I find myself staring at that seam specifically, like if I look long enough I’ll understand what it’s for before anyone has to tell me.

The other kids around me have gone quiet the same way I have, and there are a lot more of them than I let myself notice until right now, the whole yard’s worth, a couple thousand of us at least, funneled off a hundred different transports from a hundred different neighborhoods that all look like mine, every one of us doing the exact same thing with our faces. Good. At least I’m not the only one who doesn’t know what to do with his.

They walk us in through a gate none of us are given a name for, iron-grey and wide enough for equipment to pass through, not just people, and the building on the other side of it is the first thing here that looks like it was built to be seen rather than just used. Not grand. Big, which isn’t the same thing. Bare concrete pillars holding up a ceiling three stories over our heads, the kind of scale that makes two thousand kids standing around look like nothing at all inside it, and the only decoration anywhere is a long strip of names cut into the far wall, too far off to read even if I could read, running the whole length of the hall and still not reaching the end of it.

A woman with a tablet stands at the near end of it reading names off a list that means nothing to me because I can’t read the list myself, and I stand there working out which of the shapes on her screen is mine only by watching where her eyes land when she says it out loud.

“Calder.”

“Here.”

She doesn’t look up. “Dormitory four. Bunk assignment’s on the door.” A pause, the kind that means she’s already said this forty times today and will say it forty more. “Orientation on rune literacy starts tomorrow, zero-six. Don’t be late your first day, they remember that.”

I want to ask her what rune literacy actually is, and I don’t, because half the kids in this hall look like they don’t know either, and I’d rather not be the one who says so first.

Dormitory four turns out to be a long grey building with numbered doors, and mine has a card slotted into a holder at eye height with a symbol on it I can’t read, which is apparently the whole point, because the boy already inside the room when I get the door open looks up from an unpacked bag and squints at the same card like it’s personally offended him.

“You Calder?”

“Yeah.”

“Denic.” He doesn’t get up, just jerks his chin at the other bunk without looking at it, already back to digging through his bag. “That one’s yours. I called this one first, so don’t start anything, I don’t have the energy for a bunk war on day one.”

“Wasn’t planning one.”

“Smart. I’d have won anyway, I’m annoyingly hard to argue with, everyone back home says so.” He hauls a half-assembled toolkit out from under a spare set of boots, the kind of kit nobody hands out this early, the kind you’re supposed to earn a mech to justify owning, and sets it on the bunk like it’s the only thing in the bag that matters. “Don’t ask where I got this. It’s a long story and the ending makes me look worse than the beginning.”

“Wasn’t going to ask.”

“Good, because I was going to tell you anyway and I haven’t decided which lie yet.” He flips the kit open, runs a thumb down the row of tools like he’s checking none of them walked off between here and the dorm, then looks up at me properly for the first time since I came in. “You read?”

“No.”

“Nobody does. Whole room’s illiterate, apparently, that’s the entire first lesson tomorrow, and I already hate it and I haven’t even sat through it yet.” He says it with the specific bitterness of someone who’s decided in advance exactly how much he’s going to enjoy something. “You see one yet? A mech, I mean, up close, not on a screen.”

“Saw one in the yard coming in.”

“Same.” Whatever he’s about to say next, he actually stops moving to say it, hands going still over the open kit for the first time since I walked in. “Made my stomach do something it’s never done before.”

“Mine too.”

“Good. If it hadn’t, I’d have had real concerns about you as a roommate.” He closes the kit and stands, stretching until something in his back pops loud enough that I hear it from across the room. “Come on. If we don’t eat now I’m told it gets worse later, something about two thousand people and one serving line.”

The mess hall is the first place since the transport that actually sounds like this many people in one space, a wall of noise I feel more than hear before we’re even through the doors, trays clattering, a couple thousand voices doing whatever they’re doing to each other all at once. The serving line moves fast and doesn’t wait for anyone to decide what anything is before it’s on their tray, and I end up with three things I can’t name and a fourth I’m fairly sure used to be bread.

Denic drops into the first open seats he finds without checking who’s already sitting there, which turns out to be nobody, the whole table empty except us, everyone else already clustered into groups that formed somewhere between the gate and here that I apparently missed.

“First meal always looks like this,” he says, like he’s already decided that’s true rather than actually knowing it. “Empty tables. Give it a week.”

“You’ve done this before?”

“No. But I’ve got a sister who did, and she told me exactly what to expect, and I’ve decided to trust her completely on all of it except the food, because she also told me the food gets better and I refuse to believe that until I taste it myself.”

I eat without really tasting anything, still turning the day over, the socket in my pocket, the mech in the yard, the wall of names too far off to read. Around us the hall thins out in stages, whole sections emptying at once like something’s called them elsewhere, until it’s down to stragglers and the two of us, and neither of us moves to leave first.

We walk back to the dorm without talking much, the kind of quiet that isn’t uncomfortable so much as used up, both of us out of anything left to say about a day that’s already said more to us than either of us asked for.

I sit down on the bunk that’s mine now, and take the socket out of my pocket where it’s been riding all day, and turn it over once in my fingers the way I watched my mother do a hundred times without ever once asking her why.

I don’t know what it’s for either. I put it in the drawer by the bunk, the way she told me to, somewhere I won’t lose it, and I don’t think about it again for the rest of the night.
